Head-Pose Analysis

When drowsiness deepens, the head starts to drop and then jerk back up. Estimating which way the head points catches this, even when the eyes can't be seen.

Listen to this page

Trouble keeping the head up is one of the recognized warning signs of drowsy driving [1]. As the neck muscles relax, the head sags forward, and the driver often catches it with a sudden jerk: the familiar "nodding off." Head pose also helps where the eyes fail. Sunglasses can hide the eyelids completely, but they can't hide the direction of the head.

Pitch, yaw, and roll

A head's orientation is described by three rotation angles, the same ones used for aircraft:

  • Pitch: nodding up and down, rotating around the axis that runs from ear to ear. A drowsy head drop shows up here.
  • Yaw: turning left and right, as in shaking the head "no." Looking away from the road shows up here.
  • Roll: tilting the head toward a shoulder.

In a camera image, all three rotations shift where the facial landmarks appear. When the head pitches down, the nose tip moves toward the chin in the image and the eyes appear higher relative to the face outline. When it yaws, one side of the face gets narrower. Try it below.

The orange dots are the six landmarks most often used to estimate head pose: the nose tip, the chin, the outer corners of both eyes, and the two corners of the mouth. Watching how they move as you rotate the head is exactly the information an algorithm uses, run in reverse.

Estimating head pose with solvePnP

The forward direction is easy: given a 3D head and its angles, compute where the landmarks land in the image (what the sliders above do). Head-pose estimation solves the inverse: given where the landmarks appear in the image, find the rotation that puts them there. This is the Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem, and OpenCV solves it with the function cv2.solvePnP. It needs three inputs:

  1. A 3D model of where the six landmarks sit on an average human head, in any consistent unit. The same generic model works for everyone, because the goal is orientation, not an exact face shape.
  2. The 2D image positions of the same six landmarks, from Dlib or MediaPipe (Section 2).
  3. The camera's intrinsic parameters: focal length and image center. Without a calibrated camera, a good approximation is a focal length equal to the image width and a center at the middle of the image.

solvePnP searches for the rotation and translation that, when applied to the 3D model and projected through the camera, land the model points as close as possible to the detected 2D points. It returns the rotation as a compact rotation vector, which is converted to a rotation matrix with cv2.Rodrigues and then to pitch, yaw, and roll angles.

import numpy as np, cv2

# Generic 3D face model (arbitrary units): nose tip, chin, eye corners, mouth corners
MODEL_3D = np.array([
    (0.0, 0.0, 0.0),          # nose tip
    (0.0, -330.0, -65.0),     # chin
    (-225.0, 170.0, -135.0),  # outer corner of the eye on the image's left
    (225.0, 170.0, -135.0),   # outer corner of the eye on the image's right
    (-150.0, -150.0, -125.0), # mouth corner on the image's left
    (150.0, -150.0, -125.0),  # mouth corner on the image's right
])
MEDIAPIPE_IDS = [1, 152, 33, 263, 61, 291]   # same six points in MediaPipe Face Mesh

def head_pose(landmarks, w, h):
    pts_2d = np.array([(landmarks[i].x * w, landmarks[i].y * h) for i in MEDIAPIPE_IDS])
    camera = np.array([[w, 0, w / 2],
                       [0, w, h / 2],
                       [0, 0, 1]], dtype=float)      # approximate intrinsics
    ok, rvec, tvec = cv2.solvePnP(MODEL_3D, pts_2d, camera, np.zeros(4))
    rmat, _ = cv2.Rodrigues(rvec)                    # rotation vector -> matrix
    angles, *_ = cv2.RQDecomp3x3(rmat)               # matrix -> angles in degrees
    pitch, yaw, roll = angles
    return pitch, yaw, roll

A practical warning: the raw angles depend on the model's axis conventions and on where the camera is mounted, so a driver looking straight at the road rarely reads exactly 0°. Real systems record each driver's baseline pose during the first seconds of driving and measure changes from it. Draw the six points on screen while testing to confirm each index lands where expected.

Detecting nodding and head drops

Not every downward head movement is drowsiness. Drivers glance down at the speedometer, the radio, or a navigation screen all the time. The difference is in the shape of the movement over time:

  • A deliberate glance goes down smoothly, holds briefly, and comes back up at a similar, controlled speed.
  • Nodding off is a slow, uncontrolled sag as the neck muscles relax, followed by a sudden jerk back up when the driver startles awake.

That contrast gives a simple detector: flag a nod when pitch falls more than a set amount below the baseline (such as 15°) and then recovers much faster than it fell. A head that stays down past a time limit should also trigger an alert, whatever the speed. The values below are starting points to tune during testing, not standards:

PITCH_DROP = 15          # degrees below the driver's baseline
FAST_RECOVERY = 100      # degrees per second
HEAD_DOWN_LIMIT = 60     # frames (2 s at 30 fps)

def check_nod(pitch_history, baseline, fps=30):
    drop = baseline - min(pitch_history[-fps:])          # deepest point in the last second
    recovery = (pitch_history[-1] - pitch_history[-6]) * fps / 5   # deg/s over the last 5 frames
    head_down = sum(1 for p in pitch_history[-HEAD_DOWN_LIMIT:] if baseline - p > PITCH_DROP)
    if drop > PITCH_DROP and recovery > FAST_RECOVERY:
        return "NOD"                   # slow drop, sudden jerk up
    if head_down >= HEAD_DOWN_LIMIT:
        return "HEAD DOWN"             # head stayed down too long
    return "OK"

Head pose as a drowsiness indicator

Head pose is valuable in three ways.

It fills gaps. When sunglasses or glare hide the eyes, pitch still reveals a drooping head. And nodding often comes late in drowsiness, so a nod is a strong signal that an alert is overdue.

It separates drowsiness from distraction. A large, lasting yaw means the driver is looking away from the road; a pitch drop with a jerk means they are falling asleep. Zambrano et al. used pitch and yaw together with iris position to detect distraction, and only registered an event when the deviation lasted beyond a set time, so normal mirror checks didn't trigger alarms [3]. In their tests every deliberate look left, right, up, or down was caught, but drivers sitting normally were correctly recognized as facing forward only 74.07% of the time, because the pitch and yaw thresholds were sensitive to each person's distance from the camera and face shape [3]. That is the same lesson as with EAR: calibrate to the individual. The authors also note that head pose alone averages around 6° of error, and that combining it with gaze direction improves reliability [3].

It protects the other measurements. EAR is sensitive to head pose [6]: a turned head narrows the eye in the image and lowers EAR even if the eye is wide open. A system that knows the head's yaw can ignore or correct eye measurements while the head is turned, instead of mistaking a glance at the side mirror for closing eyes.

Key takeaways

  • Pitch (nodding), yaw (turning), and roll (tilting) describe head orientation; drowsy head drops appear in pitch.
  • solvePnP recovers the head's rotation from six 2D landmarks, a generic 3D face model, and approximate camera parameters.
  • Nodding off has a signature shape: a slow sag and a sudden jerk back up, unlike a deliberate glance down.
  • Head pose works when eyes are hidden, separates distraction from drowsiness, and tells the system when eye measurements can't be trusted.
