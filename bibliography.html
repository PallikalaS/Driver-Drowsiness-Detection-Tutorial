[1] Fatigued Driving: Drivers are Falling Asleep Behind the Wheel

Authors
National Safety Council (NSC)
Date
Web page, accessed October 2026
Source
nsc.org/road/safety-topics/fatigued-driver
Synopsis
A public-safety overview of drowsy driving in the United States. It gathers figures from NHTSA (633 deaths in police-reported drowsy-driving crashes in 2023, and $109 billion in yearly costs), a 2024 AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety study (drowsy drivers in 17.6% of fatal crashes from 2017 to 2021), and the CDC (about 1 in 25 adults report falling asleep while driving). It also describes microsleeps and lists the warning signs of drowsiness. Used in this tutorial for crash statistics and background.
Reliability
Medium-high. The NSC is a long-established nonprofit safety organization, and each figure on the page links to its government or research source. It is a secondary summary rather than original research, so the linked primary sources are the authority for exact numbers.

[2] A Survey on Drowsiness Detection: Modern Applications and Methods

Authors
Biying Fu, Fadi Boutros, Chin-Teng Lin, Naser Damer
Date
2024
Source
IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Vehicles. doi:10.1109/TIV.2024.3395889
Synopsis
A broad review of drowsiness detection that covers detection methods, open difficulties, and recent developments in real-time sleepiness monitoring, including where computer vision fits alongside physiological and vehicle-based approaches. Used in this tutorial for background, the overview of method families, and future directions.
Reliability
High. A peer-reviewed survey in a leading IEEE journal on intelligent vehicles, written by researchers including members of the Fraunhofer Institute for Computer Graphics Research.

[3] Driver Monitoring System Using Computer Vision for Real-Time Detection of Fatigue, Distraction and Emotion via Facial Landmarks and Deep Learning

Authors
Tamia Zambrano, Luis Arias, Edgar Haro, Victor Santos, María Trujillo-Guerrero
Date
January 2026
Source
Sensors, vol. 26, no. 3, article 889. doi:10.3390/s26030889 (open access)
Synopsis
Builds a real-time driver monitor that uses MediaPipe's 468 facial landmarks to compute the Eye Aspect Ratio, Mouth Aspect Ratio, gaze, and head pose, plus a MobileNetV2 network for emotion recognition. Tested with 27 drivers in real and simulated driving, it detected eye closure with 88.89% accuracy and yawning with 85.19%. The authors report that drivers with naturally smaller eyes triggered false drowsiness alerts under a fixed EAR threshold, and recommend per-driver calibration.
Reliability
Medium-high. Peer-reviewed, open-access journal article with a clearly described method and published results. The test group is small (27 participants from one region) and sessions were short, so results may not generalize.

[4] Real-Time Eye Blink Detection using Facial Landmarks

Authors
Tereza Soukupová, Jan Čech
Date
2016
Source
21st Computer Vision Winter Workshop (CVWW), 2016.
Synopsis
The paper that introduced the Eye Aspect Ratio: a single number computed from six landmarks around each eye that drops toward zero as the eye closes, used to detect blinks in real time. Cited here as the original source of EAR; the other references show how it is used today.
Reliability
Medium-high. A workshop paper rather than a journal article, but it is the foundational and most widely cited source for EAR, referenced by every recent paper in this bibliography.

[5] Eye Aspect Ratio for Real-Time Drowsiness Detection to Improve Driver Safety

Authors
Christine Dewi, Rung-Ching Chen, Chun-Wei Chang, Shih-Hung Wu, Xiaoyi Jiang, Hui Yu
Date
October 2022
Source
Electronics, vol. 11, no. 19, article 3183. doi:10.3390/electronics11193183 (open access)
Synopsis
Detects eye blinks in real time from dashboard-camera video using Dlib's 68 facial landmarks and the Eye Aspect Ratio. The authors tested four EAR thresholds (0.18, 0.20, 0.225, and 0.25) on their own recordings of a driver with small eyes and glasses, plus two public blink datasets. A threshold of 0.18 worked best everywhere, and accuracy fell as the threshold rose, so they recommend recalculating the threshold for each person's eye size instead of using the common default of 0.2. They report overall accuracy of 96 to 97%.
Reliability
Medium. Peer-reviewed, open-access journal article with detailed result tables. Their own dataset shows one driver, and because open-eye frames vastly outnumber closed-eye frames, the high overall accuracy hides much weaker precision and recall on the closed-eye frames in their own tables.

[6] Vision-Based Driver Drowsiness Monitoring: Comparative Analysis of YOLOv5–v11 Models

Authors
Dilshara Herath, Chinthaka Abeyrathne, Prabhani Jayaweera (University of Ruhuna, Sri Lanka)
Date
2025
Source
arXiv preprint 2509.17498. arxiv.org/abs/2509.17498
Synopsis
Fine-tunes seven YOLO object detectors (v5 through v11) on images from the UTA Real-Life Drowsiness Dataset and compares them with a classic Dlib-based EAR method. YOLOv9c was the most accurate (mAP@0.5 of 0.986), YOLOv11n gave the best balance of accuracy and speed for embedded devices, and the EAR method reached about 0.81 precision while being far cheaper to run. The authors explain that EAR's fixed threshold makes it sensitive to head pose, glasses, and lighting.
Reliability
Medium. A preprint that has not been peer reviewed, by university researchers. The method and numbers are reported in detail, but the evaluation uses a subset of 18 participants and a frame-level split.

[7] Blinking Beyond EAR: A Stable Eyelid Angle Metric for Driver Drowsiness Detection and Data Augmentation

Authors
Mathis Wolter, Julie Stephany Berrio Perez, Mao Shan
Date
November 2025
Source
arXiv preprint 2511.19519. arxiv.org/abs/2511.19519
Synopsis
Proposes the Eyelid Angle (ELA), a measure of eye openness computed from 3D facial landmarks that stays stable when the camera angle changes, unlike the 2D Eye Aspect Ratio. On public driver-monitoring datasets it showed lower variance than EAR under viewpoint changes and detected blinks accurately. The authors also use it to generate synthetic training data.
Reliability
Medium. A recent preprint that has not been peer reviewed. Included as an example of where eye-openness measurement is heading.

[8] Real-Time Deep Learning-Based Drowsiness Detection: Leveraging Computer-Vision and Eye-Blink Analyses for Enhanced Road Safety

Authors
Furkat Safarov, Farkhod Akhmedov, Akmalbek Bobomirzaevich Abdusalomov, Rashid Nasimov, Young Im Cho
Date
July 2023
Source
Sensors, vol. 23, no. 14, article 6459. doi:10.3390/s23146459 (open access)
Synopsis
Builds a real-time drowsiness detector on MediaPipe Face Mesh that combines EAR-based blink detection with iris detection: when the iris disappears and the eyelid landmarks agree, the eye is counted as closed, which reduces false blinks when the driver simply looks down. It also detects yawning and the head falling to the left, right, or back, trained on the authors' own dataset. Reported results: 95.8% for detecting drowsy (closed) eyes, 97% for open eyes, about 85% for yawning, and 98 to 100% for detecting the head falling to the side.
Reliability
Medium. Peer-reviewed, open-access journal article from Gachon University (Korea) and Tashkent State University of Economics. The authors themselves note that sunglasses can block the eye landmarks, and that without more detail on sample size, validation method, and statistics, the reliability of their accuracy figures is hard to judge fully.

[9] Real-Time Driver Drowsiness Detection Using Transformer Architectures: A Novel Deep Learning Approach

Authors
Osama F. Hassan, Ahmed F. Ibrahim, Ahmed Gomaa, M. A. Makhlouf, B. Hafiz
Date
May 2025
Source
Scientific Reports, vol. 15, article 17493. doi:10.1038/s41598-025-02111-x (open access)
Synopsis
Compares two vision transformers (ViT and Swin Transformer) against seven fine-tuned CNNs, including VGG19, DenseNet169, ResNet50V2, and MobileNet, for classifying cropped eye images as open or closed. Trained mainly on the MRL Eye Dataset (84,898 eye images), ViT reached 99.15% accuracy and Swin 99.03%, ahead of every CNN, with further tests on the NTHU-DDD and CEW datasets. In the real-time system, Haar cascades find the face and eyes, the classifier labels each eye, and an alarm sounds when a drowsiness score built from consecutive closed-eye frames passes 15. Class Activation Maps show the model focuses on the eye region.
Reliability
High for the venue: a peer-reviewed article in Scientific Reports, a Nature Portfolio journal. Two cautions: the datasets were split 80/20 at the image level, which can let near-identical images of the same person appear in both training and test sets (Section 8), and the authors note the transformers are heavier and slower than lightweight CNNs such as MobileNet.

[10] A Realistic Dataset and Baseline Temporal Model for Early Drowsiness Detection

Authors
Reza Ghoddoosian, Marnim Galib, Vassilis Athitsos (University of Texas at Arlington)
Date
2019
Source
IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition Workshops (CVPRW). doi:10.1109/CVPRW.2019.00027. Free version: arXiv:1904.07312
Synopsis
Introduces the UTA Real-Life Drowsiness Dataset: about 30 hours of self-recorded video from 60 participants in alert, low-vigilance, and drowsy states, captured on everyday phones and webcams. The authors argue that real drowsiness shows subtle signs that actors can't reproduce, report that human observers judged the videos with under 60% accuracy, and provide a baseline model based on sequences of blink features. Cited here as the original source of the dataset.
Reliability
High. A peer-reviewed workshop paper at CVPR, one of the leading computer vision conferences, and the dataset is widely used in later research, including reference [6].

[11] Driver Drowsiness Detection Dataset (NTHU-DDD)

Authors
Computer Vision Lab, National Tsing Hua University. Dataset paper: Ching-Hua Weng, Ying-Hsiu Lai, Shang-Hong Lai, "Driver Drowsiness Detection via a Hierarchical Temporal Deep Belief Network"
Date
2016 (dataset web page accessed October 2026)
Source
Asian Conference on Computer Vision (ACCV) Workshop on Driver Drowsiness Detection from Video, Taipei, Taiwan. Dataset page: cv.cs.nthu.edu.tw
Synopsis
Describes the most widely used driver drowsiness benchmark: videos of 36 subjects of different ethnicities in simulated driving, with and without glasses or sunglasses, by day and night, performing normal driving, yawning, slow blinking, falling asleep, and laughing. The training set covers 18 subjects in five scenarios, and the evaluation and test videos come from the other 18. Cited here as the original source of the dataset.
Reliability
High for describing the dataset: it is the creators' official page and paper. The dataset itself is older and the drowsiness is acted, which limits how well results transfer to real driving.

[12] EU General Safety Regulation: Driver Drowsiness and Attention Warning (DDAW) and Advanced Driver Distraction Warning (ADDW)

Authors
European Parliament and Council of the European Union; European Commission
Date
2019, with delegated regulations in 2021 (DDAW) and 2023 (ADDW)
Source
Regulation (EU) 2019/2144; Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2021/1341 (DDAW); Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2590 (ADDW). Official texts on EUR-Lex: DDAW, ADDW
Synopsis
The EU's vehicle safety law requires cars, vans, trucks, and buses to carry a driver drowsiness and attention warning system, defined as one that assesses the driver's alertness and warns them when needed: from 6 July 2022 for new vehicle types and from 7 July 2024 for all new vehicles. The technical rules allow a warning at level 7 of the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale. An advanced driver distraction warning system, which warns when the driver's visual attention leaves the driving task, is required from 7 July 2024 for new types and from 7 July 2026 for all new vehicles.
Reliability
High. These are the official legal texts published by the European Union.

[13] Laboratory for Intelligent and Safe Automobiles (LISA), UC San Diego

Authors
LISA, directed by Prof. Mohan M. Trivedi; UC San Diego Jacobs School of Engineering
Date
Lab publication list and UC San Diego news release (2017); accessed October 2026
Source
Lab publications: cvrr-nas.ucsd.edu/LISA; news release: "UC San Diego Teams with Toyota on Autonomous, Connected Vehicle Safety Technologies," jacobsschool.ucsd.edu
Synopsis
LISA studies the whole "ecology of driving," looking both at the driver and at the car's surroundings. Its published work includes driver attention monitoring, head dynamics, head pose estimation for driver awareness, and predicting driver maneuvers. The 2017 news release describes a project with Toyota's Collaborative Safety Research Center to model how control passes between an automated car and its driver.
Reliability
High for describing the lab's work: these are the lab's own publication list and an official university news release. The news release is several years old, so the lab's newest projects may differ.
