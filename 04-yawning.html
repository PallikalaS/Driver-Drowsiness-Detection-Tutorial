Mouth and Yawning Detection

A yawn is a slower, larger signal than a blink, which makes it a useful second opinion when deciding whether a driver is drowsy.

Listen to this page

Frequent yawning is one of the classic warning signs of drowsy driving [1]. It usually shows up at the drowsy stage, before the long eye closures of Section 3, and it remains visible even when the eyes are hard to measure, for example behind tinted glasses. The good news: detecting a yawn reuses exactly the same idea as EAR, applied to the mouth.

Mouth detection using facial landmarks

Both landmark models from Section 2 trace the lips in detail. In Dlib's 68-point layout, points 49 to 60 follow the outer edge of the lips and points 61 to 68 follow the inner edge, where the lips meet (indices 48 to 67 in Python). MediaPipe's 468-point mesh covers the same contours with many more points.

For yawning, the inner lip points matter most. The outer edge changes with lip thickness and smiling, but the gap between the inner lips directly measures how wide the mouth is open. A yawn opens that gap far wider than speech does.

Detecting a yawn with the Mouth Aspect Ratio

The Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR) has the same structure as EAR. Six inner-lip points are used: p1 and p4 at the corners of the mouth, p2 and p3 on the upper inner lip, and p6 and p5 directly below them on the lower inner lip.

MAR = ( ‖p2 − p6‖ + ‖p3 − p5‖ ) / ( 2 · ‖p1 − p4‖ )

Everything learned about EAR carries over: the numerator measures vertical opening at two places, the denominator measures width, and the ratio doesn't change with distance from the camera. The difference is direction. EAR falls when the eye closes; MAR rises when the mouth opens. Some systems use three vertical pairs instead of two (eight points, divided by 3 times the width), which smooths out landmark noise a little.

As with EAR, the exact numbers depend on which landmarks a system picks, so each system tunes its own threshold. With inner-lip points, a closed mouth gives a MAR near zero, normal speech produces brief values in the low tenths, and a full yawn pushes MAR well above 0.6. Those ranges are a starting point for calibration, not universal constants.

Telling a yawn apart from talking and laughing

A high MAR alone is not enough, because talking, singing, and laughing also open the mouth. Three properties separate a real yawn:

  • Height: a yawn opens the mouth much wider than speech, so MAR crosses a high threshold that talking rarely reaches.
  • Duration: a yawn holds that wide opening for seconds, while a laugh or a loud word only peaks for a moment. Requiring MAR to stay above the threshold for a minimum time, such as 1.5 seconds, filters out most false yawns.
  • Shape over time: a yawn rises slowly, holds, and falls slowly, while talking produces rapid, repeated small openings.

In code, the yawn detector is a small state machine: it notes when MAR first goes above the threshold, and when MAR drops back down it checks how long the opening lasted.

MAR_THRESHOLD = 0.6
MIN_YAWN_FRAMES = 45            # 1.5 s at 30 fps
open_frames = 0
yawn_times = []                 # timestamps of counted yawns

def update(mar, now):
    global open_frames
    if mar > MAR_THRESHOLD:
        open_frames += 1
    else:
        if open_frames >= MIN_YAWN_FRAMES:
            yawn_times.append(now)      # a real yawn just ended
        open_frames = 0                 # too short: talking or laughing

def yawns_in_last(seconds, now):
    return sum(1 for t in yawn_times if now - t <= seconds)

Yawning as a sign of drowsiness

A single yawn proves very little. People yawn when bored, when they see someone else yawn, or simply after waking up. What matters is frequency: several yawns in a short period suggest drowsiness is building. A typical design counts yawns over a sliding window, such as the last 10 minutes, and raises a "consider taking a break" warning when the count passes a limit chosen during testing.

Yawning also has practical weaknesses, which the 2026 study by Zambrano et al. ran into directly [3]:

  • Covered mouths. When testing on a dataset of driver images, their yawn detection reached only 36.84% precision, largely because faces were partly covered, often by a hand raised to the mouth [3].
  • Head position. Different natural head positions shifted the MAR measurements, reducing accuracy [3].
  • Partial yawns. In live tests, participants asked to yawn often didn't open their mouths fully, producing only small MAR changes; the system still reached 85.19% accuracy, and the authors recommend tracking MAR trends over time, the duration above threshold, and yawn counts rather than single frames [3].

Because of these weaknesses, yawning works best as a supporting signal. The eyes give the strongest evidence; yawns add confidence and fill the gap when the eyes can't be measured. Deep learning detectors can also learn yawning directly from images, alongside eye closure and head nodding [6], as Section 7 shows.

Key takeaways

  • MAR applies the EAR formula to the inner lips: it rises as the mouth opens.
  • Height alone isn't enough; requiring the opening to last about 1.5 seconds separates yawns from talking and laughing.
  • Yawn frequency over several minutes matters more than any single yawn.
  • Hands over the mouth, head position, and partial yawns limit accuracy, so yawning supports eye-based detection rather than replacing it.
