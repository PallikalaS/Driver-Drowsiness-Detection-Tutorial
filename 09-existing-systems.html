Section 9 of 12

Existing Systems

Research prototypes and production cars show what works today, and the published results show exactly where it still breaks.

Listen to this page

Camera-based drowsiness detection has moved quickly from lab experiments to legal requirement. In the European Union, every new car must now include a system that warns drowsy drivers, and a camera-based distraction warning became mandatory for all new vehicles in July 2026 [12]. This page looks at four research systems in detail, then at production systems, and closes with what the successes and failures have in common.

Examples of drowsiness detection systems

1. MediaPipe driver monitor (Zambrano et al., 2026)

Landmarks + rules, with a CNN for emotions · Ordinary USB webcam · Tested with 27 drivers [3]

How it works
MediaPipe's 468 landmarks feed EAR, MAR, head pose, and gaze measurements, combined with fixed thresholds and grace periods; a MobileNetV2 network recognizes emotions alongside [3].
Results
88.89% accuracy for eye closure and 85.19% for yawning in live tests; every deliberate look away from the road was detected [3].
Where it failed
Drivers with naturally smaller eyes were flagged as drowsy while alert; a hand over the mouth cut yawn precision to 36.84% on an image dataset; normal forward posture was recognized only 74.07% of the time because head-pose thresholds didn't fit every face and seating position [3].

2. YOLO detectors (Herath et al., 2025)

Whole-face deep learning · UTA-RLDD subset of 18 participants [6]

How it works
Seven YOLO versions trained to draw a box around the face labeled awake or drowsy, compared against a classic EAR rule [6].
Results
YOLOv9c reached the highest mAP@0.5 (0.986); the small YOLOv11n came close (0.981) and suits embedded devices; the EAR rule reached 0.812 precision [6].
Where it failed
EAR struggled with head pose, glasses, and lighting [6]. The YOLO results come from a random frame split of one dataset and weren't tested in a car, so performance on new drivers is unknown (Section 8).

3. Landmarks + iris detection (Safarov et al., 2023)

Hybrid landmark and deep learning system · Authors' own dataset [8]

How it works
MediaPipe Face Mesh landmarks give EAR and mouth measurements; iris detection confirms when an eye is really closed, reducing false blinks when the driver looks down. It also detects the head falling left, right, or back [8].
Results
95.8% for detecting drowsy eyes, 97% for open eyes, about 85% for yawning, and 98 to 100% for the head falling to the side [8].
Where it failed
The authors note that sunglasses can block the eye landmarks entirely, and they acknowledge that without more detail on sample size and validation, their accuracy figures are hard to assess [8].

4. Blink-sequence model for early drowsiness (Ghoddoosian et al., 2019)

Temporal model on blink features · UTA-RLDD, 60 participants [10]

How it works
Extracts blink features over time and feeds the sequence to a temporal model, aiming to catch subtle, early drowsiness instead of only obvious cases [10].
Results
Beat the accuracy of human observers, who themselves judged the videos correctly less than 60% of the time [10].
Where it failed
Early drowsiness is genuinely hard: the three-way split between alert, low vigilance, and drowsy remains difficult even for people [10].

Production systems in cars

Commercial driver monitoring systems (DMS) have been available on some cars for several years, often paired with hands-free highway driving features that need to confirm the driver is watching the road. They follow the same pipeline as this tutorial but with automotive hardware: a near-infrared camera mounted on the steering column or dashboard, infrared lighting so it works in darkness and through many sunglasses, and software that tracks the eyes, gaze, and head.

Regulation is now driving adoption. The EU defines a Driver Drowsiness and Attention Warning (DDAW) system as one that assesses the driver's alertness and warns them through the vehicle's displays or sounds when needed. It became mandatory for new vehicle types in July 2022 and for all new vehicles in July 2024, and the technical rules allow it to warn at level 7 of the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale, the same scale used to label training data in Section 6 [12]. A separate Advanced Driver Distraction Warning (ADDW) became mandatory for new types in July 2024 and for all new vehicles in July 2026 [12]. Because ADDW is defined around where the driver is looking, it effectively requires a driver-facing camera, which puts the eye and head tracking hardware from this tutorial into every new car sold in the EU.

Successful approaches

Looking across these systems, five design choices keep showing up in the ones that work:

  1. Fast landmark geometry for the per-frame measurements. EAR, MAR, and head pose run in real time on ordinary hardware and are easy to explain [3].
  2. Time, not single frames. Consecutive-frame counters, PERCLOS, and temporal models all beat one-frame decisions; the blink-sequence model above outperformed human observers [10].
  3. Several cues together. Eyes, mouth, and head pose cover for each other when one is hidden [3].
  4. Small, efficient networks where deep learning is used, such as YOLOv11n or MobileNetV2, so the system still runs on a phone or in-car computer [6].
  5. Infrared cameras in production systems, which remove most lighting problems before the software even starts.

Almost every failure traces back to one of two causes: the real world is more varied than the training or tuning data, or the system judges one moment instead of a trend. Section 10 looks at those real-world challenges one by one.

Key takeaways

  • Recent research systems reach roughly 85–95% on their own tests, using landmarks, CNNs, or both.
  • Their failures are consistent: fixed thresholds, covered faces, head turns, and test setups that don't match real driving.
  • In the EU, drowsiness warnings are required in all new vehicles, and camera-based distraction warnings joined them in July 2026.
  • Successful designs combine fast landmark measurements, reasoning over time, multiple cues, and infrared cameras.
